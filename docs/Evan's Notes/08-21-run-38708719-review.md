# Run 38708719 review — eval budget, model choice, attribution, synthetic quality, and speed

**Date:** 2026-08-21
**Run:** `slm-xlam-single-verify-38708719`, xlam_bfcl, single_model, COMPLETED in 5h33m
**Result:** best 0.8600 against a 0.8660 goal — 0.006 short, still improving when it ran out of evals.

The three fixes from the previous round all held. The baseline measured **0.8660** instead of 0.0000
(B313), the accuracy goal was set by the teacher's own score rather than the 0.80 floor, every rebuild
landed inside the 1,000-row ceiling, and there were zero fallbacks and zero LLM errors across ten
iterations. What follows is the review of what the run then revealed.

---

## 1. Why it stopped at 10 evaluations

I set that, in the verification launcher I wrote:

```bash
export SLM_MAX_EVALS_BEFORE_ESCALATION=10     # project default is 30
```

The default is 30 (`agent/nodes/iterate.py`), and nothing you configured changed it. I set 10 while
the interventions were still unproven, on the reasoning that ten evals is enough to watch the loader,
both rebuild sub-strategies and the curriculum growth without paying for a full run. The script
comment said as much.

That was right for the first verification run and wrong for this one. The run ended:

```
Eval cap reached: 10 evals on this model without meeting the goal (>= 10)
→ TERMINATE — single_model pins the run to one model, so there is no escalation.
best F1 : 0.8600  (threshold 0.8660)  ✗ budget exhausted
```

It was 0.006 from the goal, had improved on its two most recent kept steps, and stopped because it ran
out of budget rather than out of ideas. The cap became the binding constraint instead of the safety
net. Both verification launchers are back to 30.

Worth noting for interpretation: the 10-eval cap also sat *below* the 15-eval stagnation window, so
stagnation could never fire. The run could not have ended for the reason the loop normally ends.

## 2. Why it did not choose a smaller model

The prompt already contained **all 18 variants** — nothing was filtered. The problem was that the
information in it did not support the instruction it was given.

The instruction was "pick the MOST RESOURCE-EFFICIENT model that can plausibly reach 0.866". To act on
that, the orchestrator needs some basis for judging what a given model can reach on **this** task.
Here is every bit of evidence it had:

| variant | size | capability metrics |
|---|---|---|
| Qwen3-0.6B@Q4_K_M | 462MB | *nothing reported at all* |
| Qwen3.5-0.8B@Q4_K_M | 517MB | MMLU-Pro 29.7 |
| Qwen3-1.7B@Q4_K_M | 1223MB | MMLU-Pro 40.2 |
| Qwen3.5-2B@Q4_K_M | 1251MB | MMLU-Pro 55.3 |
| **Qwen3-4B-Instruct@Q4_K_M** | **2382MB** | **MMLU-Pro 69.6** ← chosen |
| Qwen3.5-4B@Q4_K_M | 2654MB | MMLU-Pro 79.1 |

Three things follow, and together they make the upward choice close to inevitable:

1. **MMLU-Pro does not measure function calling.** It is a general-knowledge multiple-choice
   benchmark. The orchestrator knew this was a stretch and said so, describing it as an
   "instruction-following/schema adherence proxy" — which is reaching, and it had nothing better.
2. **The metrics are base-model, zero-shot.** Every candidate is about to be LoRA fine-tuned on
   thousands of in-domain rows, which routinely lets a smaller model pass a larger one's zero-shot
   number. Extrapolating from these scores systematically overestimates how large a model is needed.
3. **MMLU-Pro rises monotonically with size, and the goal is a high number.** Given "reach 0.866" and
   an ordering whose only signal is size, the safe reading is always "go bigger". The 0.6B is worse
   than that: with nothing reported it looks strictly inferior to a model that has a score, when in
   truth one has been measured and the other has not.

It was also given the list in **pool order, unsorted**, so "pick the cheapest" required holding
eighteen sizes in mind while comparing them. Its own reason claims it chose "the smallest model
plausibly capable" — the instruction was understood; the list did not make it followable.

### What I changed

`agent/nodes/cold_start/model_selection/orchestrator_choice.py`:

- **Sorted smallest-first**, with `tier:` and an `Nx cheaper than the largest option here` ratio on
  every line. The 0.6B now reads `462MB (19.3x cheaper …)` at the top rather than buried.
- **The metrics are explained rather than just listed:** that they are general-knowledge benchmarks
  which do not measure this task, that they are base-model zero-shot numbers, and that fine-tuning
  systematically changes the picture in the smaller model's favour.
- **Unmeasured models are named as unmeasured** ("3 candidate(s) have NO published numbers …
  Unmeasured is not the same as incapable. Do not rank them last for lacking a score"), computed by
  looking for any numeric score rather than by the absence of the words "not reported".
- **The trade is stated:** the run has many iterations of data rebuilds and retuning available, so a
  model that starts short of the goal is not disqualified, whereas 5x the RAM for a benchmark score
  that does not measure the task is a bad deal. It must now also say why it rejected the cheaper
  options below its pick, which makes the reasoning auditable.

What this does **not** fix: there is still no per-task evidence for any model at selection time. The
real fix is to feed the difficulty-stratification measurements — which already score the smallest and
largest feasible models zero-shot on this exact eval set — back into selection. Today they run
*after* selection, in `eval_setup`, so they cannot inform it. Reordering that is a larger change and
is the right next step if model choice keeps skewing large.

## 3. Score attribution in the report

The growth ledger now carries the score each iteration produced, the delta the strategy caused, and a
per-strategy total, grouped by model tier. Rendered against this run:

```
  Curriculum growth and score attribution per iteration:
    ── Tier 2: Qwen/Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507@Q4_K_M  (10 iteration(s)) ──
    Iter  Strategy               Added       Curriculum    QC  Verified   Score        Δ  Kept
       1  initial_gold           +5000         0 → 4954    46         —  0.8330        —  ✓ kept
       2  hyperparameter             —             4954     —         —  0.8020  -0.0310  ✗ rolled back
       3  surgical_synthesis      +320      4954 → 5273     1   420/517  0.8070  -0.0260  ✗ rolled back
       4  mine_new_real           +982      4954 → 5925    11         —  0.8520  +0.0190  ✓ kept
       5  mine_new_real           +983      5925 → 6904     4         —  0.8320  -0.0200  ✗ rolled back
       6  surgical_synthesis      +287      5925 → 6212     0   373/448  0.8320  -0.0200  ✗ rolled back
       7  hyperparameter             —             5925     —         —  0.8480  -0.0040  ✗ rolled back
       8  hyperparameter             —             5925     —         —  0.8600  +0.0080  ✓ kept
       9  hyperparameter             —             5925     —         —  0.8270  -0.0330  ✗ rolled back
      10  mine_new_real           +882      5925 → 6802     5         —  0.8420  -0.0180  ✗ rolled back
    0 of 5 rebuild(s) added no rows

  What each strategy actually bought (kept steps only — pruned attempts were rolled back and
  contributed nothing):
    mine_new_real           +0.0190   1/3 attempt(s) kept
    hyperparameter          +0.0080   1/4 attempt(s) kept
    initial_gold             0.8330   starting point (1/1 kept)
    surgical_synthesis      +0.0000   0/2 attempt(s) kept

    FINAL                    0.8600   = 0.8330 start +0.0270 from interventions
```

Three decisions in there are load-bearing:

**The delta is measured against the last KEPT score, not the previous iteration's.** A pruned attempt
is rolled back, so the next attempt starts from the kept checkpoint. Comparing to the discarded score
would report the difference between two rejected experiments as if one had caused the other.

**Only kept steps accumulate.** A strategy's number is the accuracy the run actually *retained* from
it, not the best it transiently reached. This makes the table auditable: the contributions sum exactly
to `final − start`, so `0.8330 + 0.0270 = 0.8600` closes.

**Grouped by tier.** A run that escalates is several experiments; pooling deltas would average across
models with different ceilings. This surfaced a wiring gap — `run_health` and `run_graphics` both read
`tier` off the DAG node, and nothing ever wrote it, so both had been silently falling back to an
unlabelled heading. `evaluate_node` now records it.

It also surfaced a real off-by-one. `curate` recorded its ledger entry under the *current* iteration,
but the curriculum it writes is trained on the *next* one, so every row joined to the previous
iteration's score: the synthesis round's `+320 rows / 420-of-517 verified` was being reported against
a hyperparameter step's result. Curate now records `iteration + 1`, and the join cross-checks the
strategy on both sides — a disagreement blanks the row rather than printing another iteration's
numbers under this strategy's name.

### What the attribution says about this run

Only **mine_new_real** bought anything (+0.0190, and it is the only data intervention that ever has
on this task). **surgical_synthesis** went 0 for 2. **hyperparameter** went 1 for 4, and its one win
was +0.0080. Nine of ten attempts were rolled back — the loop is working (rollback caught every
regression) but the hit rate is low, and eight of those nine cost a full train+eval cycle at ~26
minutes each.

## 4. Synthetic data: yes, five-shot — and here is what it actually looked like

**Confirmed five-shot**, for both generation and verification, `SYNTH_SHOTS = 5`, real rows from the
task's own training split, shown as JSON in the exact output shape. The log confirms it per batch
(`new-correct (5-shot)`), and a path that cannot assemble five says so rather than quietly sending
fewer.

### Quality

The definitive answer is uncomfortable: **the synthetic rows do not exist any more.** Both synthesis
rounds regressed and were rolled back, and the next mining round overwrote `dataset_v2`, so the final
artifacts are `4954 gold + 1848 mined` and not one generated row survived on disk. That is a real
tooling gap for exactly the question worth asking, and it is now fixed —
`_archive_synthetic_rows` appends every kept row to `artifacts/synthetic_rows_audit.jsonl`, append-only
and never read back by the pipeline. Evidence, not state.

What I could still measure, from the 59 generated texts recoverable from rejection lines (a biased
sample — these are the *rejected* ones), compared against all 4,954 gold texts:

```
max-similarity to the nearest gold row: mean 0.607  median 0.594
rows >0.75 similar to an existing gold row: 4/59
worst case: 1.000  ("I need the stock prices for Intel in the US and for Volkswagen in Germany.")
```

So most rows are genuinely distinct in surface form, but one was a **verbatim copy** of a gold row and
7% were near-duplicates. Deduplication catches those downstream, so they do not enter the curriculum —
but they consumed generation and verification calls to produce nothing.

The rejection reasons are more informative than the similarity numbers, because they cluster hard:

```
Includes unrequested optional parameter 'category'.
Includes optional parameters with default values not implied by query.
Includes unrequested viewbox and omits required language parameter.
Includes optional parameter compounding_frequency which has a default value.
Includes unrequested optional parameters and wrong sort_by value.
```

Roughly half of all rejections are **one systematic error**: the generator fills in optional
parameters that the request never asked for. The orchestrator-authored brief explicitly warns against
this ("using a parameter's default value instead of the value derivable from the user text"), and the
generator did it anyway, batch after batch. That is ~20% of the synthesis budget burned on a known
mistake.

### Two changes, and why these rather than others

**Feed the actual rejections back into the next generation prompt.** `record_rejection_reasons` keeps
up to six deduplicated reasons from the last verification pass and the next generation prompt opens
with them: *"The verifier REJECTED rows from your last batch for these specific reasons. They are your
own recent mistakes on this exact task — do not repeat them."* Verifier-failure noise ("unavailable",
"unparseable") is filtered out, since those describe the verifier and not the row.

The reasoning is the same one behind five-shot prompting: a static instruction the model demonstrably
ignores is worth less than its own recent, concrete errors quoted back at it. The brief's warning is
abstract; `Includes unrequested optional parameter 'category'` is not.

**Ask for variation in substance, not wording.** The generation prompt now says: *"Vary the SUBSTANCE,
not just the wording: a different scenario, different argument values, and a different number of calls
where the task allows it. Reusing the reference's shape with new nouns adds a row the curriculum
already effectively has."* The prompt hands the teacher an anchor row and asks for the same schema
including its `tools`, so the path of least resistance is a paraphrase over the same tool set — which
is the least useful thing to add when the failing category is `wrong_arguments`.

### The honest caveat

Both synthesis rounds in this run ran through the verifier **before** the B314 fix landed on this
branch, and that verifier was rejecting ~24% of rows for invented reasons and selecting the survivors
without seeing the tool schemas. The orchestrator concluded twice that "teacher-generated argument
rows carry label/format noise". That conclusion is plausible and it is also not yet properly tested:
the filter that produced the evidence was broken. The next run is the first clean measurement.

## 5. Where the time goes, and how to make it faster

Measured from `timing-events.jsonl`, against a 5h33m wall clock:

| stage | total | n | mean | % wall |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **train** | 9,264s | 10 | 926s | **46.4%** |
| **evaluate** | 6,530s | 10 | 653s | **32.7%** |
| ├─ eval scoring | 5,484s | 13 | 422s | 27.4% |
| └─ GGUF quantize | 2,409s | 11 | 219s | 12.1% |
| eval_setup | 1,687s | 1 | — | 8.4% |
| └─ difficulty labeling | 1,376s | 1 | — | 6.9% |
| curate | 1,255s | 6 | 209s | 6.3% |
| iterate (all LLM calls) | 391s | 10 | 39s | 2.0% |

Train + evaluate is **79%** of the run. The orchestrator, which people assume is the expensive part,
is 2%.

### The bottlenecks, most actionable first

**1. Eval is strictly sequential (~27% of the run).** `training/slm_helpers.py` scores the GGUF with a
plain `for prompt in prompts:` loop, one `create_chat_completion` per row. 1,000 rows at ~0.42s each is
422s per iteration, and GPU utilisation during it will be poor — a single sequence decoding at batch
size 1 leaves an L40S almost idle. llama.cpp supports batched decoding; llama-cpp-python's chat
completion API does not expose it per call. Two ways out, and they are very different in cost:

- *Score through the vLLM server that is already running.* It is on the second GPU, it batches
  natively, and it is idle whenever synthesis is not running — which is most of the run. This is the
  large win and the larger change, because the measured artifact would no longer be the GGUF.
- *Subsample the in-loop eval.* Score 300 rows for the rollback decision and the full 1,000 only for
  a new best. Roughly a 3x cut on 27% of the run for a modest amount of extra noise in the prune
  decision. Cheap to implement, and the noise is bounded because a candidate only needs to be ranked
  against the current best, not measured absolutely.

**2. Re-quantizing every iteration (12%).** 219s × 11 to convert HF→GGUF and quantize, each time, for
a base model that never changes — only the adapter does. The clean version merges the adapter and
quantizes only when a checkpoint becomes the new best; the in-loop score comes from the bf16 LoRA. The
trade is explicit and worth stating: the in-loop number would stop being the deployed-artifact number,
and `SLM_QUANT_EVAL=1` exists precisely so that it is. Worth doing only if the quant/bf16 gap is
measured first and shown to be small on this task.

**3. Training at 926s/iteration (46%).** The least wasteful of the three — this is real work — but
the run trains from base every iteration by design, and nine of ten results were discarded. The lever
is not throughput, it is *hit rate*: with a 1-in-10 acceptance rate, most of the 46% is spent on
experiments that get rolled back. Early stopping already helps (`eval/save every 20 steps, patience 3`).
A cheaper filter before committing to a full train — a short probe on a subsample — would cut more
than any kernel-level optimisation.

**4. Difficulty labeling, 1,376s once (7%).** It scores the smallest and largest feasible models
zero-shot over the eval set. Cacheable per (task, eval-set hash, model pool) across runs, since none
of those change between runs of the same task. Pure win, no accuracy trade.

**5. One of two GPUs does the pipeline's work.** GPU 0 runs the vLLM teacher; GPU 1 runs training and
eval. During training the teacher GPU is idle; during synthesis the pipeline GPU is idle. Overlapping
them properly (prefetch the next rebuild's synthesis during training) is a scheduling change, not a
performance tweak, and it is where the last big constant factor lives.

### The single highest-value change

Not on that list: **raise the hit rate.** Nine of ten iterations were rolled back, so ~4 hours of the
5h33m produced no retained progress. Cutting eval time by 3x saves ~50 minutes; getting the acceptance
rate from 1-in-10 to 1-in-3 is worth hours. That is what §2 (better model choice) and §4 (better
synthetic data) are actually for.

## 6. Also fixed

- `!! final report failed: SystemExit: 0` — the last line of an entirely successful run. My
  `_emit_final_report` wrapper caught `BaseException`, which includes the `SystemExit(0)` that signals
  a clean finish. It now re-raises `SystemExit` and only reports genuine failures.
- The DAG now records `tier`, which both the attribution table and the accuracy chart had been reading
  and never finding.
